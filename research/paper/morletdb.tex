\title{Recordings, Not Rows: Lossless Waveform Storage and Lag-Tolerant Retrieval Inside PostgreSQL}
\subtitle{Working draft 0.5 --- not for distribution}
\author{Zin Nam}
\affiliation{\institution{}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
Vibration, seismic and physiological archives hold uniformly sampled recordings, yet relational systems store them as rows of samples or as detached feature vectors. We describe \M{}, a PostgreSQL extension that stores each recording as losslessly coded chunks with transactional, replay-safe appends, keeps search representations as replaceable materialized views of those chunks, and returns search hits as sample intervals that read back bit-exactly. We evaluate it with frozen protocols on six real sensor inputs and on 28 station-weeks of continuous seismic data. Three findings reach beyond the prototype. First, many sensor floats are calibrated integers, and on two calibrated inputs the tested ALP implementation fell back to about 60 bits per sample; grid recovery with last-place corrections, given a nominal quantum, needed 9.2--15.5, 1.5--1.6$\times$ less than the best metadata-free arm tested; most of the remaining gap to integer codecs is correction cost. Second, storing the same envelopes in pgvector costs identical space, but zero-lag cosine search shares only 65\% of the lag-tolerant top ten: lag tolerance changes the query, not just its speed. Third, on 235 thousand seismic windows, exhaustive in-database lag-tolerant search grew linearly over the measured range at about 12\,\textmu s per window, and a zero-lag shortlist did not substitute for it: re-ranking the 1000 nearest windows by zero-lag cosine recovered 74\% of the exhaustive top ten, whether the shortlist was exact or from HNSW. Because each lag term of the score is an inner product with a zero-padded shifted query, a grid of 13 shifted probes through an ordinary inner-product HNSW index recovers the bounded-lag top ten: on stations and a year no earlier study had read, recall@10 was 0.976 at a p95 of 131\,ms against 2,854\,ms exhaustively, and the indexes stayed current inside the append transaction during replayed ingestion. Under metadata filters, routing small eligible galleries to the exact scan and restricting probes to eligible recordings kept recall at 0.977--1.0 where post-filtering had fallen to 0.52. Materialized envelopes cost 12.9\% of raw storage on event windows but 60\% on continuous data with half-overlapping windows. We also report the negative results that shaped the design: wavelet and progressive codecs, cross-recording reference coding and a Morlet transform all failed to beat simpler alternatives.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Condition monitoring, seismology and clinical physiology produce long, uniformly sampled recordings. An analyst first finds an interval of interest, then asks where similar behaviour occurred and what the original samples show. That workflow needs three things at once: compact storage of the original samples, a search representation, and a reliable link from every hit back to its samples and acquisition context.

Relational systems offer two layouts, each covering part of the problem. One row per sample makes every value addressable but costs about 93 bytes per sample uncompressed in our measurements; compressed time-series extensions shrink this but treat samples as independent floats. A vector per window gives a compact search key, but the application must maintain the link to the source samples, keep representations compatible, and decide what similarity means. Neither layout captures a \emph{recording}: a channel with a sample rate, a unit, a calibration and a half-open sample coordinate system.

\M{} is a PostgreSQL extension (Rust, via pgrx) built around that unit. Recordings are appended in chunks at global sample offsets; raw chunks, derived representations and checkpoints commit in one transaction; and every search result is a recording identifier plus a sample interval that reads back through the same extension. The design rests on one premise: \emph{authoritative samples and search representations should be stored separately, but connected by explicit interval semantics and transactional ingestion}. The name reflects the first representation tried, Morlet wavelet energies; our experiments later showed no advantage over FFT band powers (Section~\ref{sec:negative}), and the representation is now a versioned, replaceable part.

This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A recording-oriented design inside PostgreSQL}: replay-safe chunk appends at global offsets, lossless chunk coding, materialized and replaceable search representations, and interval read-back, under PostgreSQL transactions and permissions (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
\item \textbf{A storage characterization of calibrated sensor floats} on identical samples against ALP, Parquet, zstd, FLAC and DuckDB (Section~\ref{sec:storage}). On two calibrated inputs, the tested ALP implementation fell back to near-raw size; with a declared quantum, recovering the digitizer grid reduced size 1.5--1.6$\times$ below zstd; most of the remaining gap to integer codecs is the cost of reproducing the source's floating-point rounding.
\item \textbf{Lag-tolerant retrieval with standard vector indexes}: precision of materialized envelopes; equal storage but unequal queries against pgvector; a diagnosis that zero-lag shortlists fail because of their candidate metric, not because of approximate search; and \emph{shifted-probe search}, which retrieves bounded-lag candidates through an inner-product HNSW index and re-ranks them exactly, confirmed on held-out data and maintained transactionally during ingestion
(Section~\ref{sec:retrieval}).
\item \textbf{Negative results that shaped the system}, each from a frozen protocol (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{enumerate}
We do not claim a new entropy coder, a new similarity measure, or a lag-tolerant index with guarantees: probe search is approximate and evaluated empirically. Code, protocols, raw results and the commit at which each protocol was frozen are public~\cite{repo}.

\section{Data model and semantics}\label{sec:model}
A \emph{recording} is a sequence $x_0,\ldots,x_{N-1}$ of float64 values with a sample rate $f_s$, a channel, a unit, a declared sample quantum $q$ (Section~\ref{sec:codec}) and JSON acquisition metadata. An \emph{interval} $[a,b)$ denotes $x_a,\ldots,x_{b-1}$. Sample offsets are authoritative; wall-clock time additionally needs a time origin, and a real acquisition gap starts a new recording rather than silently joining samples.

A search representation $\phi_v(x_{a:b})$ is identified by a versioned extractor name $v$. Two windows are comparable only if their recordings share sample rate, channel and unit and their windows share length and extractor; metadata containment filters restrict candidates further. These rules reject comparisons that are computable but meaningless, such as distances between features from different sample rates.

We separate three notions of correctness that are often conflated. \emph{Sample identity}: decoding returns the appended float64 bit patterns. \emph{Search fidelity}: a stored or approximate representation reproduces a reference ranking, measured here by recall@10. \emph{Task relevance}: retrieved intervals help answer a domain question. Each needs its own evidence; fidelity says nothing about relevance. We call results \emph{source-linked} in the operational sense that each hit carries its recording, interval and metadata; this is not cryptographic provenance.

\subsection{Collections and transactional appends}
A collection is a set of caller-owned tables created by \code{morlet_create_stream_collection}: recordings with their checkpoint, raw chunks, and per-representation tables (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). All functions run as \code{SECURITY INVOKER}, so PostgreSQL privileges and row-level security apply to every read and search. An append names its global start offset:
\begin{lstlisting}
SELECT morlet_start_recording('signals', 'pump7', 25600,
  'accel_x', 'g', '{"asset":"P7"}', 512, 256, 'zscore',
  'none', 0.001);                       -- quantum q
SELECT morlet_append_chunk('signals', 'pump7', 0, :x);
SELECT * FROM morlet_search_envelope_interval('signals',
  'pump7', 81920, 86016, '{"asset":"P7"}', 10);
SELECT samples FROM morlet_read_interval('signals',
  'pump7', 81920, 86016);
\end{lstlisting}
Raw samples, derived representation rows and the checkpoint commit in one transaction, so a committed checkpoint always refers to committed samples. An append that repeats a committed range with identical samples is verified and accepted as a no-op; a changed retry, a gap or an overlap fails. Collectors can therefore retry after a crash without their own deduplication. Windows of derived representations may cross chunk boundaries.

\subsection{Lossless chunk coding}\label{sec:codec}
Each raw chunk is one \code{bytea} value. The codec combines established techniques: fixed polynomial predictors of order 0--4 as in Shorten and FLAC~\cite{shorten,flac}, partitioned Rice coding of zig-zag mapped residuals~\cite{rice}, and a CRC-32C checksum. Its distinctive part is how it treats floats.

Most sensor values are calibrated integers: a digitizer emits counts $d$, and acquisition software stores $x=f(d)$, for example $x=d\cdot s$ (seismic counts, WAV) or, in EDF~\cite{edf}, $x=(d-d_{\min})(p_{\max}-p_{\min})/(d_{\max}-d_{\min})+p_{\min}$. The recording declares a nominal quantum $q$: 1 for counts, 0.001 for three decimals, the ADC step for calibrated values. Per chunk, the encoder seeks a grid
\begin{equation}
x_i \;=\; o + k_i\,s \;\oplus\; \delta_i,
\end{equation}
with integer indices $k_i$, a step $s$ refined from $q$ to within one unit in the last place (ulp), an offset $o$ (format version 3; zero in version 2), and corrections $\delta_i$, stored as differences of order-preserving integer images of float64, so that decoding reproduces every bit pattern. A division mode $x=k/d$ covers decimal steps exactly. Indices are predicted and Rice-coded like integers; corrections form a second stream. If no grid fits within a thousandth of a step, the chunk is coded as order-preserving float bits, and if that is not smaller, as raw float64. The quantum is a storage hint, never a conversion: a wrong quantum costs space, not accuracy.

\subsection{Materialized, replaceable search representations}
Search representations are rows derived from raw chunks and keyed by recording, window size and start sample. We study the envelope representation: for a 4096-sample window, z-score, take the magnitude of the analytic signal, smooth with a 50-sample moving mean, keep every tenth value, and z-score again. This gives 410 values, stored as float16 (821 bytes with a version byte). The distance between a query envelope $q$ (full precision) and a stored envelope $g$ is
\begin{equation}
D(q,g) \;=\; 1-\max_{|\ell|\le L}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t} q_{t+\ell}\,g_t ,\qquad L=\lfloor 0.075\,n\rfloor=30,
\end{equation}
a biased cross-correlation over bounded shifts. Search is an exhaustive scan of eligible envelopes scored in Rust (\code{morlet_envelope_distance}, eight lags per inner block), issued as one statement that PostgreSQL can run with parallel workers, with the source interval excluded and overlapping windows of one recording suppressed greedily in rank order. Materialization and float16 storage change values relative to envelopes computed from raw samples; Section~\ref{sec:retrieval} measures the effect. Dropping and rebuilding a representation never touches raw chunks.

\textbf{Probe search.} Each lag term of $D$ is an inner product: with the zero-padded shifted query $s_\ell[t]=q_{t+\ell}$ for $0\le t+\ell<n$ (else 0), $\sum_t q_{t+\ell}g_t=\langle s_\ell,g\rangle$, so $1-D(q,g)=\max_\ell \langle s_\ell,g\rangle/n$. An optional, pgvector-backed table holds a float16 copy of the envelopes with an inner-product HNSW index. \code{morlet_search_envelope_probes} issues probes at lags $-L,-L{+}5,\dots,L$ (13 at $n=410$), takes 50 candidates per probe, and re-ranks their union with the exact scorer under the same eligibility, exclusion, ordering and suppression; returned distances are exact, only the candidate set is approximate. Probes must use inner product rather than cosine, because $\|s_\ell\|$ differs between lags. \code{morlet_build_envelopes} writes each new window's vector row in the same transaction, and rows reference their envelopes with cascading deletes. pgvector remains optional: the objects that use it are created on demand. Filters are handled by routing: when at most a threshold of windows (default 5,000) is eligible, the exact scan runs; otherwise each probe scans only eligible recordings with pgvector's iterative index scan (Section~\ref{sec:ss3}).

\subsection{Codec against modern baselines (B1)}
B1 compares codecs on identical samples, each verified lossless~\cite{b1}. Float codecs receive only float64 values: zstd level 19 per chunk, Parquet with \code{BYTE_STREAM_SPLIT} and zstd, and DuckDB 2.0's automatic float compression, which uses ALP and its fallback ALP-RD~\cite{alp,duckdb}. \M{} receives the values plus the nominal quantum. The Parquet float arm is one fixed encoding, not a tuned Parquet baseline; ALP is DuckDB 2.0.0's implementation, measured as table blocks.

Figure~\ref{fig:storage} and Table~\ref{tab:b1} show three regimes.

\emph{Counts and decimals.} When values are integers or short decimals (CX, IMS, Paderborn counts, ECG with gain 1000), ALP and \M{} both recover integers; \M{} is 20--51\% smaller than ALP, plausibly because consecutive samples are correlated and prediction exploits that, while ALP's frame-of-reference bit-packing does not; B1 does not isolate this. \M{} is within 5\% of FLAC, whose LPC stage it lacks, and slightly below FLAC on ECG.

\emph{Calibrated floats.} On Paderborn physical values and Sleep-EDF, no decimal exponent turns values into integers, and the tested DuckDB ALP implementation selected ALP-RD, storing about 60 bits per sample, near raw. Of the three metadata-free arms, zstd was smallest. \M{}, which additionally receives the nominal quantum, was 1.5--1.6$\times$ smaller than zstd. Our pre-registered claim of at least 2$\times$ was not met.

\emph{The correction gap.} Given counts, \M{} codes the two calibrated inputs at 7.9 and 8.8 bits per sample; given the calibrated floats, it needs 1.3 and 6.7 bits per sample more. That extra cost, relative to \M{}'s own integer-input path, is the correction stream: the source software computed $x$ with a particular sequence of roundings (on Sleep-EDF a multiplication, a division and an addition), which a single fitted step and offset does not reproduce for every sample. It does not explain the whole difference from FLAC, which also includes \M{}'s integer path being 0.15--0.18 bits per sample larger than FLAC's. Replaying the source's calibration arithmetic might remove the corrections when the correct parameters and operation sequence are known; this is neither implemented nor tested. Our pre-registered claim that \M{} stays within 15\% of the best integer codec held on four of six inputs and failed on these two.

For float compression, these inputs illustrate that real-valued sensor data can be neither decimal nor arbitrary but an affine image of integers under floating-point rounding. On the two such inputs we tested, the decimal-oriented ALP implementation fell back to near-raw size and the best byte-oriented arm was 1.5--1.6$\times$ larger than coding with a declared quantum.

Float16 envelopes met all frozen criteria (Table~\ref{tab:r1b}): recall@10 of 0.9999, 12.9\% of the raw table, and an unchanged catalogue-distance relevance proxy ($+0.001$\,km, 95\% interval $[-0.002, +0.004]$). \subsection{Same storage, different query (P1)}
P1 stored the identical float16 envelopes in a pgvector \code{halfvec(410)} column~\cite{pgvector}. Both tables occupied exactly 1,794,048 bytes, and all 1,826 envelopes were bit-identical, so envelope storage itself is no advantage. Zero-lag cosine search, however, shared only 64.7\% of the lag-tolerant top ten and reproduced its order for 0.1\% of queries. Scoring lags in the client on the same \code{halfvec} rows reproduced \M{}'s ranking exactly. In a later same-run comparison, median latency was 6.85\,ms for \M{}, 0.49\,ms for zero-lag cosine and 20.3\,ms for client lag scoring. The first two answer different questions, so their ratio is not a like-for-like speed comparison.

\subsection{Scaling and zero-lag shortlists (R2, R2-D)}\label{sec:r2}
R2 asked how exact search scales and whether a standard approximate index can serve as its first stage~\cite{r2}. We loaded CX HHZ at stations PB01, PB02, PB06 and PSGCX for 1--14 March 2025, downloaded as continuous station-days for R2 (48 short windows, 0.1\% of these samples, had been read by an earlier study): 481\,M samples in 128 gap-free recordings, appended in 451\,s and stored at 6.2 bits per sample. Envelopes over 4096-sample windows at stride 2048 gave 234,762 windows (275\,s to build). Galleries were nested by day, from 4,217 to 234,762 windows. The 100 queries were 50 random and 50 high-energy windows from the first day. A shortlist arm takes the top $K$ windows by zero-lag cosine on a pgvector \code{halfvec} copy of the same float16 envelopes and re-ranks them with the exact lag-tolerant distance in the database, under the same exclusion and suppression. R2 intended its shortlist to come from an HNSW index ($m$ 16, \code{ef_construction} 64), with a pre-registered bar of recall@10 $\ge 0.95$ at no more than a tenth of the exact p95 latency on the largest gallery. A diagnostic follow-up on the same data (R2-D, protocol frozen before measuring) found that R2's statement never used the index: its query vector came from a subquery, which pgvector cannot use as an index ordering key, so PostgreSQL computed an exact zero-lag top $K$. R2-D therefore compares an explicitly exact shortlist (E-$K$, plan-checked sequential scan) with the HNSW index actually used (H$^*$-$K$, query vector as a parameter, plan-checked index scan).

Over the measured range, exact search time grew linearly at 12.1--12.8\,\textmu s per stored window (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}): 54\,ms for 4,217 windows, 0.86\,s for 67,472 and 2.8\,s for 234,762. These figures are for the original implementation, which iterated over candidates with a PL/pgSQL cursor that PostgreSQL never parallelizes; fetching the best rows in one parallel statement and suppressing overlaps on those (fetching more if suppression exhausts them) later returned identical lists with bitwise-equal distances about 2.5$\times$ faster (unfiltered p50 3.3 to 1.3\,s on a loaded machine; one station 0.78 to 0.31\,s). Interpolating between the two smallest galleries places the 100\,ms mark near 8,000 windows; no gallery of that size was measured.

No shortlist arm met the bar (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}). With $K{=}1000$, recall@10 fell from 0.977 on the smallest gallery, where 1000 candidates are a quarter of it, to 0.743 on the largest; with $K{=}100$, from 0.714 to 0.605. The HNSW shortlist agreed with the exact one on 98--100\% of candidates on the largest gallery and gave the same recall to within 0.007 at every size measured (0.744 for H$^*$-1000). Every shortlist returned $K$ candidates, and every member of the exhaustive top ten that a shortlist contained survived re-ranking, exclusion and suppression, so coverage equalled recall. The loss is therefore attributable to the zero-lag candidate metric at these budgets, not to approximate search or to the suppression rules: a quarter of the lag-tolerant top ten lies outside the 1000 nearest windows by zero-lag cosine. The exact zero-lag top ten alone shared 43--47\% of the exhaustive top ten on these continuous recordings, below P1's 64.7\% on event windows. These are measurements for two budgets and one HNSW configuration.

With the index in use, the HNSW top 100 plus re-ranking took a median 26\,ms on the largest gallery, against 2.8\,s exhaustively, at recall 0.60; the top 1000 took 0.23\,s at recall 0.74. Re-ranking dominates the latter.

\textbf{Representation overhead.} The materialized envelopes took 12.9\% of the raw chunk table in R1b but 60.2\% in R2. Measured sizes account for the factor of 4.68: halving the stride from 4096 (one envelope per event window) to 2048 doubles the number of envelopes per sample ($\times2.0$); R2's continuous, mostly ambient recordings compress to 5.04 payload bits per sample against 12.39 for R1b's event windows ($\times2.46$); row and TOAST overheads differ slightly ($\times0.95$ combined). The ratio thus depends on stride, raw compressibility and physical layout, and the 12.9\% figure does not transfer to continuous archives. R2's benchmark held four structures side by side: raw chunks (371\,MB, always required), envelopes (223\,MB, used by the exhaustive scan and every re-rank), the \code{halfvec} copy (241\,MB) and its HNSW index (275\,MB). The copy stores the same values as the envelopes, so a deployment would keep one of the two if its scorer read that format (\M{}'s reads its own); the index is needed only for an index-based shortlist.


\subsection{Shifted-probe search (BL1)}\label{sec:bl1}
BL1 asked whether candidate generation that respects the lag range recovers the exhaustive top ten~\cite{bl1}. Its protocol fixed grids of 7 and 13 probes (spacing 10 and 5) and 50 or 100 candidates per probe, re-ranked by the unchanged scorer, with controls that separate descriptor quality from approximate search: the same probes scored exactly (no ANN), and an exact zero-lag shortlist given the same number of candidates. The gate required recall@10 $\ge 0.95$, p95 at most a third of the exhaustive scan's and below the strongest exact baseline, and added storage at most 1.5$\times$ the raw chunk table. The strongest exact baseline was a single parallel SQL statement scoring every eligible envelope, 3--4$\times$ faster than the PL/pgSQL scan with identical results.

On R2's development data all four configurations passed; the selected one (13 probes, 50 each) reached recall 0.998 at a p95 of 115\,ms against 2,835\,ms exhaustively and 954\,ms for the parallel exact scan, re-ranking 338 windows on average. We froze it and evaluated it once on CX stations PB05, PB11, PB14 and PB18, 1--14 March 2021: stations and a year that no earlier study in the repository had read, chosen from the availability service alone before any waveform was downloaded.

A router, fixed on development data before the confirmation run, sends a query to exact search when at most 5,518 windows are eligible (where exact p95 reached 100\,ms) and to filter-aware probes otherwise.

\subsection{Storage of search representations (SS1)}\label{sec:ss1}
With probe search enabled every window is stored twice: as the scorer's \code{bytea} envelope and as the index's \code{halfvec} copy, which hold the same float16 values in 98.3\% of positions. SS1 tested whether one copy suffices~\cite{ss1}. A single \code{halfvec} table holding the stored bits was exact on both databases (no mismatching value among 469,384 windows, no unequal distance among about 9.4 million pairs, identical top-ten lists) and saved about 250\,MB per 235,000 windows, 0.49--0.67$\times$ the raw data, but made exact scans 23\% slower through per-row format conversion. An HNSW index on an expression over the existing envelope table kept exact search unchanged and was equally exact, but raised probe p95 by 27--32\% (about 20\,ms), most likely because the expression is re-evaluated for every returned row. Both missed their pre-registered latency bounds, so the two-copy layout remains the default; the expression index is a valid storage-saving operating point when 20\,ms matter less than 250\,MB.

\subsection{Building representations (CW1)}\label{sec:cw1}
As a first step toward compaction-style workers that run representation work outside the database, CW1 compared building envelopes inside PostgreSQL with stateless external processes that read encoded chunks through SQL, compute with the same Rust code, and write rows back with key-idempotent inserts~\cite{cw1}. On 33,736 windows the external path produced byte-identical rows in every run, survived a killed and restarted worker and two workers processing the same recordings, and moved 90\% of the per-window CPU off the database server (0.13 against 1.31\,ms of database CPU per window). It also used about four times less CPU in total, which exposed the real inefficiency: the in-database builder issued one SQL statement per window and decoded every raw sample twice. Batching the in-database builder, so that each chunk is decoded once for up to 256 windows, reduced database CPU to 0.31\,ms per window and raised one session from 740 to 3,084 windows per second (four sessions: 12,034), byte-identical to before. External workers remain a viable basis for separating compute from storage, but on one machine batching delivered most of their benefit without them.

\section{Application evidence and negative results}\label{sec:negative}
\textbf{Sleep staging by example (S1).} On 153 Sleep-EDF nights with a subject-disjoint split, staging each 30-second epoch by the 40 most similar expert-scored epochs from other subjects gave a mean per-subject Cohen's $\kappa$ of 0.583, against 0.541 for logistic regression on the same features ($+0.042$, 95\% CI $[0.016, 0.068]$), with equal macro-F1~\cite{sleep}. Retrieval thus exposes source-linked neighbours at no measured accuracy cost against this simple comparator; part of the $\kappa$ difference comes from the regression's class-balanced weights. The result is far from state-of-the-art staging and makes no clinical claim.

\textbf{Morlet versus FFT.} In the same study, FFT band powers with the same band layout gave $\kappa$ 0.589 against 0.583 for Morlet energies. We found no advantage for the wavelet representation and made the extractor replaceable.

\textbf{Wavelet and progressive storage.} A compression benchmark found the tested wavelet decomposition codec 19--57\% \emph{larger} than scalar quantization with conventional coding at matched error bounds, and progressive raw storage missed its byte-saving gate (0.59 against $\le 0.5$ of a cascade) because a separate envelope index dominated~\cite{compression}. This motivated storing a retrieval representation \emph{next to} losslessly coded raw data rather than a multiresolution raw format.

\textbf{Reference coding.} Coding a machining recording as a residual against an earlier execution of the same operation would have saved at most 0.0019\% on the Bosch CNC data~\cite{bosch}, because executions were too dissimilar sample by sample~\cite{x1}.

\textbf{Out of scope.} Three later studies tested retrieval-based forecasting of bearing and turbofan degradation and current-to-vibration estimation; none passed its gate~\cite{repo}. They concern analytics on top of the store rather than storage and retrieval.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Float compression.} Gorilla~\cite{gorilla} and Chimp~\cite{chimp} XOR consecutive values and suit slowly changing metrics; ALP~\cite{alp} finds decimal exponents and falls back to ALP-RD for high-precision doubles; FCBench~\cite{fcbench} compares such methods across domains. Our results add a data class to this picture: calibrated sensor floats. On the two such inputs we tested, a declared quantum with per-chunk step refinement was smaller than the tested ALP implementation and than zstd; whether replaying calibration arithmetic can remove the remaining corrections is open.

\textbf{Waveform and audio coding.} Shorten and FLAC~\cite{shorten,flac} established polynomial or LPC prediction with Rice coding~\cite{rice}; seismology stores counts with Steim compression in miniSEED~\cite{seed}. \M{} reuses these techniques and adds the float grid with exact corrections.

\textbf{Time-series and array stores.} TimescaleDB~\cite{timescale} compresses rows of samples in PostgreSQL, with Gorilla-style coding for floats. SciDB~\cite{scidb} and TileDB~\cite{tiledb} manage dense arrays outside a relational engine. Columnar formats such as Parquet~\cite{parquet} and engines such as DuckDB~\cite{duckdb} store sample columns well (Table~\ref{tab:b1}) but leave recording semantics, replay-safe appends and interval read-back to the application.

\textbf{Similarity search.} FAST~\cite{fast} fingerprints seismic waveforms for large-scale similarity search; the UCR suite~\cite{ucr} and matrix-profile methods~\cite{matrixprofile} search subsequences under shift and warping. Graph indexes such as HNSW~\cite{hnsw}, available in PostgreSQL through pgvector~\cite{pgvector}, give approximate nearest neighbours under fixed metrics. k-Shape's shape-based distance~\cite{kshape} and MASS~\cite{mass} evaluate normalized cross-correlation over all shifts exactly, the latter with per-subsequence normalization unlike \M{}'s biased, bounded-lag score; FAST obtains shift tolerance on the data side by indexing densely overlapping windows. Streaming vector indexes such as FreshDiskANN~\cite{freshdiskann} and VStream~\cite{vstream} keep zero-lag ANN indexes fresh under updates. Among the sources we read we found none that issues query-side shifted probes into an inner-product ANN index to recover a bounded-lag cross-correlation top-$k$; we do not claim the general idea of multi-probe search as new. \M{}'s contribution is the placement of an exact lag-tolerant scorer inside the database next to the samples it links back to, a candidate-generation method for it that uses standard indexes, and their measurement against these alternatives.

\section{Limitations}
\textbf{One machine, warm caches.} All timings come from one laptop-class machine, one client and warm caches; we did not measure disk-cold or concurrent workloads. R2 and R2-D tested one HNSW configuration and two candidate budgets; R2's own HNSW arm silently ran as an exact scan because no plan check covered it, which R2-D found and corrected. Probe search was evaluated on galleries of about 235,000 windows; filtered search was evaluated at five filter levels on the same two databases, and the router missed its pre-registered latency bound at the unfiltered level of the confirmation data by 0.8\,ms. Stage B used one day of four channels and one worker. Storage sizes are deterministic and transfer to other machines; latencies do not.

\textbf{Baselines.} TimescaleDB was tested in one configuration; ALP was measured inside DuckDB, including its block layout; FLAC is the only LPC-based integer codec. The Parquet float arm used one fixed encoding.

\textbf{Data reuse and relevance.} P1 reuses R1b's data, the P2 inputs come from earlier studies, an earlier engineering check had measured \M{}'s storage of the Sleep-EDF collection, and R2-D is a diagnostic on R2's data. R1b's 2022 windows, BL1-C's March 2021 slice of four never-used CX stations and S1's held-out subjects had not been used before; R2's March 2025 slice overlapped 0.1\% of its samples with windows an earlier study had read. Our main retrieval metric is fidelity (recall against an exact reference). Task relevance is supported only by a catalogue-distance proxy for seismic events and by sleep staging; we make no claim about other domains or workloads.

\textbf{Correctness.} Bitwise round trips on every measured chunk, replay tests and permission checks are engineering evidence, not formal verification of the codec, the transaction logic or access control.

\section{Conclusion}
Treating a recording, rather than a sample or a vector, as the unit of storage lets a relational engine keep original samples compact, keep search representations replaceable, and return results that read back exactly. On the two calibrated inputs tested, declaring the digitizer grid mattered more than codec sophistication: where the tested ALP implementation stayed near 60 bits per sample, \M{} needed 9--16, and most of the remaining gap to integer codecs is the cost of reproducing the source's rounding. Whether replaying calibration arithmetic removes it is untested. For retrieval, an ordinary vector column stores envelopes equally well, but lag tolerance changes the answer: over the measured range the exhaustive scan grew linearly, and a zero-lag shortlist of 1000, exact or from HNSW, recovered three quarters of its top ten. Probing the lag terms directly, as inner products with shifted queries, recovered 97.6\% of the bounded-lag top ten on held-out data at a twentieth of the exhaustive latency, through an index PostgreSQL users already have, and kept new windows searchable at commit. Materialized representations are cheap relative to raw data only when windows are sparse or raw data compresses poorly. Two engineering results matter as much as the algorithm: filters need routing, because post-filtering an approximate candidate set silently loses recall when few windows qualify; and building representations in batches, decoding each raw chunk once, cut database CPU per window 4.2$\times$ with byte-identical output, which is most of what moving the work to external compute workers would have bought. Keeping a single stored representation is exact and saves a third of the search storage, but on PostgreSQL either design costs 20--30\% latency on one search path. Exact replay of calibration arithmetic, and relevance beyond scorer fidelity, remain open.
