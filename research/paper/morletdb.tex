\title{Recordings, Not Rows: Lossless Waveform Storage and Lag-Tolerant Retrieval Inside PostgreSQL}
\subtitle{Working draft 0.2 --- not for distribution}
\author{Zin Nam}
\affiliation{\institution{}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
Vibration, seismic and physiological archives hold uniformly sampled recordings, yet relational systems store them as rows of samples or as detached feature vectors. We describe \M{}, a PostgreSQL extension that stores each recording as losslessly coded chunks with transactional, replay-safe appends, keeps search representations as replaceable materialized views of those chunks, and returns search hits as sample intervals that read back bit-exactly. We evaluate it with frozen protocols on six real sensor inputs and on fresh seismic data. Three findings generalize beyond the prototype. First, sensor floats are usually calibrated integers, and decimal-oriented float codecs miss this: on two calibrated inputs ALP falls back to about 60 bits per sample, while grid recovery with last-place corrections needs 9.2--15.5, 1.5--1.6$\times$ less than the best metadata-free codec tested; the remaining gap to integer codecs is entirely correction cost. Second, storing the same envelopes in pgvector costs identical space, but zero-lag cosine search shares only 65\% of the lag-tolerant top ten: lag tolerance changes the query, not just its speed. Third, on 235 thousand fresh seismic windows, exact in-database lag-tolerant search costs a predictable 12\,\textmu s per window, and an HNSW index on the same envelopes is not a usable prefilter: re-ranking its top 1000 recovers only 74\% of the exact top ten. We also report the negative results that shaped the design: wavelet and progressive codecs, cross-recording reference coding and a Morlet transform all failed to beat simpler alternatives.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Condition monitoring, seismology and clinical physiology produce long, uniformly sampled recordings. An analyst first finds an interval of interest, then asks where similar behaviour occurred and what the original samples show. That workflow needs three things at once: compact storage of the original samples, a search representation, and a reliable link from every hit back to its samples and acquisition context.

Relational systems offer two layouts, each covering part of the problem. One row per sample makes every value addressable but costs about 93 bytes per sample uncompressed in our measurements; compressed time-series extensions shrink this but treat samples as independent floats. A vector per window gives a compact search key, but the application must maintain the link to the source samples, keep representations compatible, and decide what similarity means. Neither layout captures a \emph{recording}: a channel with a sample rate, a unit, a calibration and a half-open sample coordinate system.

\M{} is a PostgreSQL extension (Rust, via pgrx) built around that unit. Recordings are appended in chunks at global sample offsets; raw chunks, derived representations and checkpoints commit in one transaction; and every search result is a recording identifier plus a sample interval that reads back through the same extension. The design rests on one premise: \emph{authoritative samples and search representations should be stored separately, but connected by explicit interval semantics and transactional ingestion}. The name reflects the first representation tried, Morlet wavelet energies; our experiments later showed no advantage over FFT band powers (Section~\ref{sec:negative}), and the representation is now a versioned, replaceable part.

This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A recording-oriented design inside PostgreSQL}: replay-safe chunk appends at global offsets, lossless chunk coding, materialized and replaceable search representations, and interval read-back, under PostgreSQL transactions and permissions (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
\item \textbf{A storage characterization of calibrated sensor floats} on identical samples against ALP, Parquet, zstd, FLAC and DuckDB (Section~\ref{sec:storage}). Calibrated floats defeat decimal-oriented float codecs; recovering the digitizer grid closes most of the gap to integer codecs; the remaining gap is the cost of reproducing the source's floating-point rounding.
\item \textbf{An analysis of lag-tolerant retrieval in a relational engine}: precision of materialized envelopes, equal storage but unequal queries against pgvector, a scaling study on fresh data showing that exact lag-tolerant scans are linear and that a standard vector index is not a sufficient prefilter for them
(Section~\ref{sec:retrieval}).
\item \textbf{Negative results that shaped the system}, each from a frozen protocol (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{enumerate}
We do not claim a new entropy coder, a new similarity measure or a sublinear lag-tolerant index. Code, protocols, raw results and the commit at which each protocol was frozen are public~\cite{repo}.

\section{Data model and semantics}\label{sec:model}
A \emph{recording} is a sequence $x_0,\ldots,x_{N-1}$ of float64 values with a sample rate $f_s$, a channel, a unit, a declared sample quantum $q$ (Section~\ref{sec:codec}) and JSON acquisition metadata. An \emph{interval} $[a,b)$ denotes $x_a,\ldots,x_{b-1}$. Sample offsets are authoritative; wall-clock time additionally needs a time origin, and a real acquisition gap starts a new recording rather than silently joining samples.

A search representation $\phi_v(x_{a:b})$ is identified by a versioned extractor name $v$. Two windows are comparable only if their recordings share sample rate, channel and unit and their windows share length and extractor; metadata containment filters restrict candidates further. These rules reject comparisons that are computable but meaningless, such as distances between features from different sample rates.

We separate three notions of correctness that are often conflated. \emph{Sample identity}: decoding returns the appended float64 bit patterns. \emph{Search fidelity}: a stored or approximate representation reproduces a reference ranking, measured here by recall@10. \emph{Task relevance}: retrieved intervals help answer a domain question. Each needs its own evidence; fidelity says nothing about relevance. We call results \emph{source-linked} in the operational sense that each hit carries its recording, interval and metadata; this is not cryptographic provenance.

\subsection{Collections and transactional appends}
A collection is a set of caller-owned tables created by \code{morlet_create_stream_collection}: recordings with their checkpoint, raw chunks, and per-representation tables (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). All functions run as \code{SECURITY INVOKER}, so PostgreSQL privileges and row-level security apply to every read and search. An append names its global start offset:
\begin{lstlisting}
SELECT morlet_start_recording('signals', 'pump7', 25600,
  'accel_x', 'g', '{"asset":"P7"}', 512, 256, 'zscore',
  'none', 0.001);                       -- quantum q
SELECT morlet_append_chunk('signals', 'pump7', 0, :x);
SELECT * FROM morlet_search_envelope_interval('signals',
  'pump7', 81920, 86016, '{"asset":"P7"}', 10);
SELECT samples FROM morlet_read_interval('signals',
  'pump7', 81920, 86016);
\end{lstlisting}
Raw samples, derived representation rows and the checkpoint commit in one transaction, so a committed checkpoint always refers to committed samples. An append that repeats a committed range with identical samples is verified and accepted as a no-op; a changed retry, a gap or an overlap fails. Collectors can therefore retry after a crash without their own deduplication. Windows of derived representations may cross chunk boundaries.

\subsection{Lossless chunk coding}\label{sec:codec}
Each raw chunk is one \code{bytea} value. The codec combines established techniques: fixed polynomial predictors of order 0--4 as in Shorten and FLAC~\cite{shorten,flac}, partitioned Rice coding of zig-zag mapped residuals~\cite{rice}, and a CRC-32C checksum. Its distinctive part is how it treats floats.

Most sensor values are calibrated integers: a digitizer emits counts $d$, and acquisition software stores $x=f(d)$, for example $x=d\cdot s$ (seismic counts, WAV) or, in EDF~\cite{edf}, $x=(d-d_{\min})(p_{\max}-p_{\min})/(d_{\max}-d_{\min})+p_{\min}$. The recording declares a nominal quantum $q$: 1 for counts, 0.001 for three decimals, the ADC step for calibrated values. Per chunk, the encoder seeks a grid
\begin{equation}
x_i \;=\; o + k_i\,s \;\oplus\; \delta_i,
\end{equation}
with integer indices $k_i$, a step $s$ refined from $q$ to within one unit in the last place (ulp), an offset $o$ (format version 3; zero in version 2), and corrections $\delta_i$, stored as differences of order-preserving integer images of float64, so that decoding reproduces every bit pattern. A division mode $x=k/d$ covers decimal steps exactly. Indices are predicted and Rice-coded like integers; corrections form a second stream. If no grid fits within a thousandth of a step, the chunk is coded as order-preserving float bits, and if that is not smaller, as raw float64. The quantum is a storage hint, never a conversion: a wrong quantum costs space, not accuracy.

\subsection{Materialized, replaceable search representations}
Search representations are rows derived from raw chunks and keyed by recording, window size and start sample. We study the envelope representation: for a 4096-sample window, z-score, take the magnitude of the analytic signal, smooth with a 50-sample moving mean, keep every tenth value, and z-score again. This gives 410 values, stored as float16 (821 bytes with a version byte). The distance between a query envelope $q$ (full precision) and a stored envelope $g$ is
\begin{equation}
D(q,g) \;=\; 1-\max_{|\ell|\le L}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t} q_{t+\ell}\,g_t ,\qquad L=\lfloor 0.075\,n\rfloor=30,
\end{equation}
a biased cross-correlation over bounded shifts. Search is an exhaustive scan of eligible envelopes scored in Rust (\code{morlet_envelope_distance}, eight lags per inner block), with the source interval excluded and overlapping windows of one recording suppressed greedily in rank order. Materialization and float16 storage change values relative to envelopes computed from raw samples; Section~\ref{sec:retrieval} measures the effect. Dropping and rebuilding a representation never touches raw chunks.

\subsection{Codec against modern baselines (B1)}
B1 compares codecs on identical samples, each verified lossless~\cite{b1}. Float codecs receive only float64 values: zstd level 19 per chunk, Parquet with \code{BYTE_STREAM_SPLIT} and zstd, and DuckDB 2.0's automatic float compression, which uses ALP and its fallback ALP-RD~\cite{alp,duckdb}. \M{} receives the values plus the nominal quantum. Integer codecs receive the exact digitizer counts: FLAC~\cite{flac}, Parquet delta coding with zstd, and DuckDB bit-packing. Two inputs are new to our storage studies: a Sleep-EDF EEG slice~\cite{sleepedf,physionet} from 32 nights with symmetric and asymmetric calibration ranges, and PTB-XL ECG lead~I~\cite{ptbxl}.

Figure~\ref{fig:storage} and Table~\ref{tab:b1} show three regimes.

\emph{Counts and decimals.} When values are integers or short decimals (CX, IMS, Paderborn counts, ECG with gain 1000), ALP and \M{} both recover integers; \M{} is 20--51\% smaller than ALP because consecutive samples are correlated and prediction exploits that, while ALP's frame-of-reference bit-packing does not. \M{} is within 5\% of FLAC, whose LPC stage it lacks, and slightly below FLAC on ECG.

\emph{Calibrated floats.} On Paderborn physical values and Sleep-EDF, no decimal exponent turns values into integers, so DuckDB chose ALP-RD and stored about 60 bits per sample, near raw. The best metadata-free codec was zstd. \M{} with the nominal quantum was 1.5--1.6$\times$ smaller than zstd. Our pre-registered claim of at least 2$\times$ was not met.

\emph{The correction gap.} Given counts, \M{} codes the two calibrated inputs at 7.9 and 8.8 bits per sample; given the calibrated floats, it needs 1.3 and 6.7 bits per sample more. The difference is the correction stream. The source software computed $x$ with a particular sequence of roundings (on Sleep-EDF a multiplication, a division and an addition), which a single fitted step and offset cannot reproduce for every sample. A grid that replays the source's calibration arithmetic exactly would remove the corrections; we have not built it. Our pre-registered claim that \M{} stays within 15\% of the best integer codec held on four of six inputs and failed on these two.

The lesson for float compression is that ``real-valued'' sensor data is often neither decimal nor arbitrary: it is an affine image of integers under floating-point rounding. Decimal-oriented codecs fail on it, and generic byte-oriented ones leave most of the savings unused.

\subsection{Precision of materialized envelopes (R1b)}

The reference decodes raw samples and computes envelopes in the client.

Float16 envelopes met all frozen criteria (Table~\ref{tab:r1b}): recall@10 of 0.9999, 12.9\% of the raw table, and an unchanged catalogue-distance relevance proxy ($+0.001$\,km, 95\% interval $[-0.002, +0.004]$). The roughly 80-fold latency reduction measures materialization against a client that decodes and recomputes; it is not a speedup over an optimized raw-scanning engine.

\subsection{Same storage, different query (P1)}
P1 stored the identical float16 envelopes in a pgvector \code{halfvec(410)} column~\cite{pgvector}. Both tables occupied exactly 1,794,048 bytes, and all 1,826 envelopes were bit-identical, so envelope storage itself is no advantage. Zero-lag cosine search, however, shared only 64.7\% of the lag-tolerant top ten and reproduced its order for 0.1\% of queries. Scoring lags in the client on the same \code{halfvec} rows reproduced \M{}'s ranking exactly. In a later same-run comparison, median latency was 6.85\,ms for \M{}, 0.49\,ms for zero-lag cosine and 20.3\,ms for client lag scoring. The first two answer different questions, so their ratio is not a like-for-like speed comparison.

\subsection{Scaling and approximate prefiltering (R2)}\label{sec:r2}
R2 asked how exact search scales and whether a standard approximate index can serve as its first stage~\cite{r2}. We loaded 14 days of four CX stations from 2025, data no earlier study had read: 481\,M samples in 128 gap-free recordings, appended in 451\,s and stored at 6.2 bits per sample. Envelopes over 4096-sample windows at stride 2048 gave 234,762 windows (275\,s to build). Galleries were nested by day, from 4,217 to 234,762 windows. The 100 queries were 50 random and 50 high-energy windows from the first day. Arm H-$K'$ took the top $K'$ windows by zero-lag cosine from a pgvector HNSW index ($m$ 16, \code{ef_construction} 64) on the same float16 envelopes and re-ranked them with the exact lag-tolerant distance in the database; arm Z used the cosine order alone. The pre-registered bar for the prefilter was recall@10 $\ge 0.95$ at no more than a tenth of the exact p95 latency on the largest gallery.

Exact search was linear at 12.1--12.8\,\textmu s per stored window at every size (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}): 54\,ms for 4,217 windows, 0.86\,s for 67,472 and 2.8\,s for 234,762. It stays interactive (under 100\,ms) up to about 8,000 windows, which in practice means search filtered to one station-day or one machine.

The prefilter failed its bar. With $K'{=}1000$, recall@10 fell from 0.977 on the smallest gallery, where 1000 candidates are a quarter of it, to 0.743 on the largest, at 0.28\,s; with $K'{=}100$ it fell to 0.61. Zero-lag cosine alone shared 43--47\% of the exact top ten on these continuous recordings, below the 64.7\% of P1's event windows. Random and high-energy queries behaved alike. The lag-tolerant neighbours of a window are thus not concentrated among its zero-lag neighbours, and a fixed candidate budget captures a shrinking share of them as the gallery grows. The index also costs more than the data: side table and HNSW took 516\,MB, 1.4$\times$ the raw samples, while the envelopes themselves took 0.60$\times$ (more than R1b's 12.9\% because half-overlapping windows store every sample in two envelopes).

Lag-tolerant search therefore needs either a filter that keeps galleries in the thousands, which the metadata predicates provide, or a shift-invariant first-stage key; an off-the-shelf vector index over the shifted representation is not one.

\section{Application evidence and negative results}\label{sec:negative}
\textbf{Sleep staging by example (S1).} On 153 Sleep-EDF nights with a subject-disjoint split, staging each 30-second epoch by the 40 most similar expert-scored epochs from other subjects gave a mean per-subject Cohen's $\kappa$ of 0.583, against 0.541 for logistic regression on the same features ($+0.042$, 95\% CI $[0.016, 0.068]$), with equal macro-F1~\cite{sleep}. Retrieval thus exposes source-linked neighbours at no measured accuracy cost against this simple comparator; part of the $\kappa$ difference comes from the regression's class-balanced weights. The result is far from state-of-the-art staging and makes no clinical claim.

\textbf{Morlet versus FFT.} In the same study, FFT band powers with the same band layout gave $\kappa$ 0.589 against 0.583 for Morlet energies. We found no advantage for the wavelet representation and made the extractor replaceable.

\textbf{Wavelet and progressive storage.} A compression benchmark found the tested wavelet decomposition codec 19--57\% \emph{larger} than scalar quantization with conventional coding at matched error bounds, and progressive raw storage missed its byte-saving gate (0.59 against $\le 0.5$ of a cascade) because a separate envelope index dominated~\cite{compression}. This motivated storing a retrieval representation \emph{next to} losslessly coded raw data rather than a multiresolution raw format.

\textbf{Reference coding.} Coding a machining recording as a residual against an earlier execution of the same operation would have saved at most 0.0019\% on the Bosch CNC data~\cite{bosch}, because executions were too dissimilar sample by sample~\cite{x1}.

\textbf{Out of scope.} Three later studies tested retrieval-based forecasting of bearing and turbofan degradation and current-to-vibration estimation; none passed its gate~\cite{repo}. They concern analytics on top of the store rather than storage and retrieval.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Float compression.} Gorilla~\cite{gorilla} and Chimp~\cite{chimp} XOR consecutive values and suit slowly changing metrics; ALP~\cite{alp} finds decimal exponents and falls back to ALP-RD for high-precision doubles; FCBench~\cite{fcbench} compares such methods across domains. Our results add a data class to this picture: calibrated sensor floats, for which a declared quantum and per-chunk step refinement beat decimal search, and for which exact replay of calibration arithmetic remains open.

\textbf{Waveform and audio coding.} Shorten and FLAC~\cite{shorten,flac} established polynomial or LPC prediction with Rice coding~\cite{rice}; seismology stores counts with Steim compression in miniSEED~\cite{seed}. \M{} reuses these techniques and adds the float grid with exact corrections.

\textbf{Time-series and array stores.} TimescaleDB~\cite{timescale} compresses rows of samples in PostgreSQL, with Gorilla-style coding for floats. SciDB~\cite{scidb} and TileDB~\cite{tiledb} manage dense arrays outside a relational engine. Columnar formats such as Parquet~\cite{parquet} and engines such as DuckDB~\cite{duckdb} store sample columns well (Table~\ref{tab:b1}) but leave recording semantics, replay-safe appends and interval read-back to the application.

\textbf{Similarity search.} FAST~\cite{fast} fingerprints seismic waveforms for large-scale similarity search; the UCR suite~\cite{ucr} and matrix-profile methods~\cite{matrixprofile} search subsequences under shift and warping. Graph indexes such as HNSW~\cite{hnsw}, available in PostgreSQL through pgvector~\cite{pgvector}, give approximate nearest neighbours under fixed metrics. \M{}'s contribution is not the measure but its placement, a lag-tolerant scorer inside the database next to the samples it links back to, and its measurement against these alternatives.

\section{Limitations}
\textbf{One machine, warm caches.} All timings come from one laptop-class machine, one client and warm caches; we did not measure disk-cold or concurrent workloads. R2 tested one HNSW configuration; larger graphs might raise recall, but not beyond the ranking disagreement that zero-lag search shows. Storage sizes are deterministic and transfer; latencies do not.

\textbf{Baselines.} TimescaleDB was tested in one configuration; ALP was measured inside DuckDB, including its block layout; FLAC is the only LPC-based integer codec. A codec that replays calibration arithmetic exactly would change the calibrated-float comparison.

\textbf{Data reuse and relevance.} P1 reuses R1b's data and the P2 inputs come from earlier studies; R1b, R2 and S1 used data no earlier study had read. Our main retrieval metric is fidelity (recall against an exact reference). Task relevance is supported only by a catalogue-distance proxy for seismic events and by sleep staging; neither establishes usefulness for industrial troubleshooting.

\textbf{Correctness.} Bitwise round trips on every measured chunk, replay tests and permission checks are engineering evidence, not formal verification of the codec, the transaction logic or access control.

\section{Conclusion}
Treating a recording, rather than a sample or a vector, as the unit of storage lets a relational engine keep original samples compact, keep search representations replaceable, and return results that read back exactly. On calibrated sensor floats, declaring the digitizer grid mattered more than codec sophistication: it turned a 60-bit fallback into 9--16 bits, and exact replay of calibration arithmetic is the remaining step to integer-codec size. For retrieval, an ordinary vector column stores envelopes equally well, but lag tolerance changes the answer, and a standard vector index over the same envelopes cannot stand in for the exact lag-tolerant scan, which is linear and interactive only on filtered galleries of thousands of windows. A shift-invariant index, and exact replay of calibration arithmetic, are the two open problems these results point to.
